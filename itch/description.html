January 1945. The Luftwaffe's General Staff proposes to send about a hundred Mistel, pilotless Ju 88 bombers steered by a fighter on their backs, against thirteen power stations of Moscow and the Upper Volga. Three weeks later its own operations staff proposes to postpone the attack. Both memos survive in the US National Archives.

Iron Hammer, 1945 is a hypothetical campaign study built on that file. It does not ask whether the attack could have won the war. It follows the staff's own arithmetic, shows what that arithmetic left out, and sets the Soviet side beside it.

Two ways through the same eight chapters.

  • Read the study: a dossier with the file in facsimile, full transcription and English translation, the data, the Soviet power system that had already survived worse in 1941, and a critical reflection. At the end, the file's own plan of 18 January is computed.
  • Take the staff's seat: decide on targets, weapon, jump-off airfields, fuel and method, and on 7 February whether to attack at all, while the front swallows one airfield after another. A model runs your plan with the file's figures, Soviet figures from the record and named assumptions, and sets the result against both.
  • Extreme what-if (optional): a handful of He 177 bombers with a Fw 190 on top, a project that never flew. Every figure for it is marked as an assumption.

A source finding. The usual story says Eisenhammer was abandoned after a US raid on Rechlin destroyed 18 Mistel. The file shows the 18 as combinations delivered, and the proposal to postpone was made in February, two months before that raid.

Every figure is linked. The file's numbers, the Soviet numbers and the study's assumptions are marked as such. Plates: public-domain photographs of Mistel combinations taken by the US Army as its troops overran Bernburg, Merseburg and Gardelegen in 1945. No swastika is shown; where one appears on a photograph, it is masked.
